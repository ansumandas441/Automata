Automata · pipeline teardown

Two recordings go in. One edited video comes out.

Nothing in the middle looks at pixels. The system listens to what you said, watches where you looked, writes both down as timestamped facts, and then a plain state machine decides the edit. Here is every step, traced against a real 9-minute run.

What is actually on the two tapes

Everything downstream depends on measuring the inputs rather than assuming them. Two properties of this pair mattered, and neither was visible without checking.

Property screen.mp4 camera.mp4 Consequence
Duration 558.040 s 552.635 s Editable window is the overlap: 552.635 s. The screen's last 5.40 s is dropped.
Frame rate 25.000 23.976 Both normalised to 25 fps before compositing. Output matches the screen, whose text shows judder most.
Audio level −91 dB −24.6 dB The screen track exists but is digital silence. Speech is on the camera, so that is the transcription source.
Start time 0.000 0.000 Both clips share t=0, so offset_s is 0.0 for each.
The master clock. Every timestamp in the system lives on one shared timeline: master = local + offset_s. Wall-clock time is used exactly once, to derive that offset. After that everything reads container PTS, never frame counts — dropped frames would silently drift a frame-counting clock apart over nine minutes.

Speech — what was said, and exactly when

Whisper transcribes the camera's audio with word-level timings, gated on voice activity. The gate is not an optimisation: over silence the model confidently invents phrases like “Thanks for watching!”, and a screencast is full of quiet typing stretches. 

{ "type": "utterance", "t": 0.14, "end": 0.7, "text": "My friends,",
  "words": [ { "text": "My",       "t": 0.14, "end": 0.34 },
             { "text": "friends,", "t": 0.34, "end": 0.70 } ] }

Raw Whisper segments are bounded by breath, not by sentences — one will happily return “No wait, that is wrong. Cut that. Alright, back to me.” as a single block. Those word timings are used to re-split each segment at sentence boundaries, so a later “cut that” removes one sentence rather than three.

Gaze — are you addressing the lens or the screen

A face landmarker samples the camera eight times a second, reading head pose and eye direction. Frames are located by presentation timestamp, never by index. Each sample is one of three states, and the third one carries the most weight. 

{ "type": "gaze", "t": 0.0, "state": "away", "confidence": 0.605 }

looking   299   eyes on the lens
away      602   face visible, attention elsewhere
unknown  3516   no face found at all
Unknown is not away. A missing face means the person leaned out of frame, the light dropped, or a hand crossed the lens. Reporting that as “looked away” would swing the camera every time they reach for coffee. Unknown holds the current shot instead — which is why 80% unknown here produced a long steady shot rather than chaos.

Intent — did you instruct the editor

Each sentence is labelled with one of four values. A free local pattern matcher tries first; only genuinely ambiguous lines are worth escalating to a language model, and every verdict is cached to disk so a re-run produces the identical edit. 

focus_screen   "let me show you this function"
focus_camera   "back to me for a second"
cut_previous   "cut that"  — an instruction, not the verb
neutral        ordinary narration — 201 of 201 on this run

The model never decides the edit. It labels one sentence and stops. That keeps the result reproducible, roughly fifty times cheaper, and debuggable — when a cut is wrong you can name the rule that made it.

02

The bus puts time back in order

The producers run at wildly different rates — gaze at 8 Hz, speech in bursts, a language-model verdict arriving a second or more after the sentence it describes. Events therefore arrive out of order.

Consuming them in arrival order is exactly how an auto-editor cuts the wrong sentence. So each producer declares a watermark meaning “I will never emit anything earlier than this”, and the bus releases events only once every producer has passed that point. The director then receives a strictly ordered stream, and asserts that ordering rather than trusting it.

03

The director decides, deterministically

This is the whole product. A fixed state machine consumes the ordered events and holds one piece of state: which layout is live right now. Four rules compete, highest priority first.

Forced
A source went missing. Nothing to composite against, so this bypasses every damper below.
Explicit
You said so. A spoken instruction pins the layout for six seconds.
Gaze
Where you are looking, once it has held steady for 0.8 s.
Hold
Change nothing. The default, and what unknown gaze resolves to.

Two dampers sit on top, and they matter more than classifier accuracy ever will: no shot may be shorter than 2.5 s, and gaze must hold for 0.8 s before it may move the camera. Without them, someone glancing between screen and lens produces a strobing, unwatchable edit.

The eyes arrive at 23.77. The cut happens at 24.65 — 0.88 s later, the debounce refusing to act on what might have been a glance. Then the eyes leave again at 25.15, only half a second into the new shot. An undamped director would cut straight back, producing a 0.5 s flash. Instead the minimum-shot rule holds the camera until 27.40.

Every decision is labelled. Each shot carries the rule that produced it — default, gaze on lens, said: back to me, camera source lost. When an edit looks wrong, that field is usually the entire investigation.

Two clocks live in this file. segments are output time, after cuts. kept_spans are master time — which stretches of the original survive. With no cuts on this run the two coincide, but the moment you say “cut that” they diverge, and confusing them is the fastest way to hand-edit a timeline wrongly.
05

One ffmpeg command does all the pixels

project.timeline.json → automata render → out.mp4 231 MB · 38 s

The renderer is deliberately dumb. It normalises both inputs to the output canvas and frame rate, trims them to the kept spans, then stacks three overlays whose timing comes from enable= expressions. 

# normalise, trim, concat — both sources
[0:v]fps=25,scale=1920:1080,pad=…,setsar=1[screen_src];
[1:v]fps=25,scale=1920:1080,pad=…,setsar=1[camera_src];

# camera fills the frame during the five camera shots
[screen_base][camera_base]overlay=0:0:enable='between(t,24.650,27.402)
  +between(t,35.661,43.794)+between(t,72.447,74.950)
  +between(t,534.534,540.790)+between(t,543.543,546.045)'[comp1];

# the corner insets, each on the complementary spans
[comp1][camera_pip]overlay=W-w-36:36:enable='…'[comp2];
[comp2][screen_pip]overlay=W-w-36:H-h-36:enable='…'[comp3];
The graph never grows. Timing lives inside those expressions rather than in one filter chain per shot, so a forty-minute video with three hundred shot changes builds exactly the same graph as a forty-second one. Only the expressions get longer.

The result was verified rather than assumed: the finished file was transcribed again and its speech timings compared against the events file. They agreed to within about 50 ms, which confirms the frame-rate conversion and the master-clock mapping both survived the render.

—

What each file is for

The three commands are separate because their costs are wildly different. Analysis is slow and non-deterministic; planning is instant and pure. That split is what makes tuning practical.

project.json
Your inputs and settings. Edit the director block to change how often it switches; the render block for size and inset corners.
project.events.json
What perception observed. Hand-correct a mistranscribed line here rather than re-recording. Re-running analyze refuses to overwrite it without --force.
project.timeline.json
The edit itself. Change a layout to override one moment, then render with -t. Save under a new name — re-planning overwrites this.
out.mp4
The finished video. Fully reproducible from the other three at any time.

Every figure on this page comes from one real run: a 9 minute 13 second recording, analysed in about four minutes, planned in milliseconds, rendered in 38 seconds. Gaze thresholds remain uncalibrated against a real face — the one part of the system still waiting on proper footage.
